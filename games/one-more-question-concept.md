# One More Question

Status: untested game concept

## Hypothesis

`One More Question` is a simple-to-medium social strategy game about coordinating a game night. Players must collectively agree on where to meet, when to meet, which game to play, what must be brought, and which rulebooks or references must be read. Each player secretly wants the gathering to happen, but also wants to be the person whose logically defensible concerns delay it the longest.

The design hypothesis is that ordinary scheduling friction can become a legible negotiation game when every objection must be supported by visible constraints and every delay risks destroying the shared event.

## Shared objective

Before the planning deadline, the group must produce one complete Game Night Plan:

- A date and start time everyone can attend.
- A venue that satisfies capacity, travel, accessibility, and closing-time requirements.
- A selected game compatible with player count and available duration.
- An assigned owner for the game, components, food, and transportation.
- A rules-reading plan identifying the authoritative rulebook and each reader's responsibility.

If the group fails to schedule a viable game night, everyone loses.

## Personal objective

Among the players who remain credible and available for the final plan, the winner is the player who caused the greatest justified delay.

A delay is justified only when it exposes a real contradiction or unmet dependency, such as:

- The venue closes before the selected game can finish.
- A player cannot reach the venue at the proposed time.
- The game does not support the confirmed player count.
- Nobody has accepted responsibility for teaching a required rules section.
- Two required components or responsibilities have been assigned incompatibly.

Repeated objections, invented constraints, contradictions, and objections raised after their evidence was already visible cost Credibility. A player at zero Credibility cannot win even if their objections delayed the plan.

## Core play loop

Each round represents one planning exchange:

1. Reveal a new shared fact, obligation, or complication.
2. Every player simultaneously chooses to Propose, Accept, Question, Delegate, or Research.
3. Resolve proposals and objections against the visible planning board.
4. Add valid dependencies to the plan and reject invalid arguments.
5. Advance the calendar when the group has not completed a viable plan.
6. Check whether the current plan is complete, consistent, and unanimously acceptable.

Players may negotiate, trade responsibilities, promise preparation, or form temporary coalitions. No player may transmit a private constraint after it has ceased to be logically relevant merely to manufacture delay.

## Information and components

The initial prototype should use:

- One Planning Board with Date, Time, Venue, Game, Attendance, Supplies, and Rules tracks.
- Constraint cards for schedules, travel, accessibility, preferences, and obligations.
- Game cards listing player count, duration, complexity, owner, and reading requirements.
- Venue cards listing capacity, hours, cost, travel, food, and accessibility.
- Responsibility cards for Host, Teacher, Rules Reader, Transport, Food, and Component Check.
- Credibility tokens and Delay points.
- A shared calendar or deadline track.

Most information should be public. Each player may hold one private constraint and one private preference, but both must have precise reveal conditions. This keeps deduction bounded and prevents arbitrary take-that play.

## Scoring

When a valid Game Night Plan is completed:

- Gain Delay points for each accepted objection that forced the plan to change.
- Gain additional points when an objection revealed a dependency involving multiple plan elements.
- Gain Credibility for resolving another player's valid objection.
- Lose Credibility for contradictions, duplicate objections, irrelevant concerns, and broken commitments.
- Players at zero Credibility are ineligible to win.
- The eligible player with the most Delay points wins.

The scoring system must reward useful obstruction rather than noise. The best move should be the latest possible truthful intervention that materially improves the final plan.

## Complexity target

The intended weight is simple-to-medium:

- One shared board and one repeated action grammar.
- Mostly public information.
- Few resources: Credibility, Delay, and calendar position.
- Strategic depth from timing, dependencies, negotiation, and selective disclosure.
- A complete first prototype should support three to six players.

Complexity, duration, and player-count quality are design targets, not measured claims.

## First prototype questions

1. Can players distinguish justified delay from performative obstruction without a moderator?
2. Does the shared-loss condition keep the group moving toward an actual plan?
3. Is delaying more valuable when done late, or does that create frustrating surprise losses?
4. How much private information is needed before deduction becomes interesting?
5. Do rules-reading responsibilities create genuine dependencies or merely bookkeeping?
6. Can the game remain funny through recognizable incentives without relying on scripted jokes?
7. Does the completed plan feel like a satisfying collective artifact?

## Promotion boundary

This file records only a game hypothesis. The concept should not be described as playable until it has a rules draft, component inventory, deterministic setup examples, end-condition tests, and recorded prototype sessions.
